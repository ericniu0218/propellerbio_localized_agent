Human clinical evidence

RSV is the leading cause of hospitalization in infants.[7][5][3] RSV infection continues to aggravate in part of RSV-infected patients.[3] RSVpreF resulted in 50% neutralizing titers peaking 2 weeks post-vaccination.[8] Maternal smoking exposure was reported in 27.0% of mothers.[1] nirsevimab coverage was 94.0% among eligible newborns.[1]

Prevention-specific evidence

RSVpreF vaccination resulted in 50% neutralizing titers peaking 2 weeks post-vaccination.[8] Among the immunized neonates with available data on maternal smoking exposure, 395 mothers (27.0%) reported any exposure to tobacco smoke during pregnancy, whereas 1067 (73.0%) had no exposure.[1] One case occurred in the immunized group and one in the non-immunized group.[1] The median age at administration was 3.0 days (IQR 2-6 days).[1] Breakthrough RSV infections occur despite RSV vaccination or monoclonal antibody injection.[9] While prevention measures are available, there is no effective drug treatment for RSV.[7] These findings support further evaluation of EDP-323 for prophylaxis.[9]

Negative and conflicting results

There is no effective drug treatment for RSV.[7] RSV-infected patients cannot be controlled and continue to aggravate.[3] Maternal smoking exposure was not independently associated with NICU admission risk.[1] Rare coding variants present in homozygous form were prioritized.[10] Breakthrough RSV infections occur despite RSV vaccination or monoclonal antibody injection.[9] Crude products were purified by recrystallization with ethanol or by flash chromatography.[5] The longer side chain of N169 in subtype B enhances its interaction to the light chain of 5B11.[2] F832/33 did not significantly reduce cell viability at concentrations up to 25 µM.[4] The mechanism regulating the discrepancy of underlying pathology processes after RSV infection is still obscure.[3] One case occurred in the immunized group and one in the non-immunized group.[1]
